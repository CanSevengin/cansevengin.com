The EU's new €2 handling fee is a fixed charge per item on parcels sold to EU consumers from outside the EU, and it is set to apply from 1 November 2026. It comes on top of the €3 customs duty per item that has applied to parcels worth €150 or less since 1 July. For a small exporter, that means up to €5 of fixed cost per product line before shipping, and that changes which products still make sense to send one by one.

I've spent most of my career selling cross-border, and one lesson keeps coming back: fixed costs per order decide your product range more than any strategy deck does.

## What actually changed

Until this summer, goods worth €150 or less could enter the EU without customs duty. VAT was already due, but duty wasn't. That exemption ended on 1 July 2026, replaced by a temporary flat duty of €3 per item on low-value consignments in distance sales to consumers. It is meant to run until 1 July 2028, when the EU Customs Data Hub is supposed to go live and normal duty rates take over.

On 21 September 2026 the European Commission adopted a delegated regulation fixing a separate Union handling fee at €2 per item. It is set to apply from 1 November 2026. The European Parliament and the Council have a short window to object before the act is published, so the figure is set but not final in law until then. Unlike the €3 duty, the handling fee also applies to distance sales above €150.

## How the EU €2 handling fee is counted

"Per item" is the detail that matters. An item is not a single unit and not a parcel. It is a group of goods in the same shipment that share the same tariff classification (and origin, where relevant).

The Commission's own example: five T-shirts in one parcel count as one item. A T-shirt and a watch count as two. So:

- one product type in a parcel: €3 duty + €2 fee = €5
- three different product types: €9 + €6 = €15

Quantity doesn't move the number. Variety does.

## Who feels it most

Do the rough math on a €15 product shipped on its own: €5 of fixed charges is about a third of the price, before VAT, postage and marketplace commission. On a €60 product, the same €5 is closer to 8%. The higher the order value, the less this hurts.

That's why I think the people who should pay the most attention are:

- **Micro-exporters and small brands** shipping single, low-priced items directly to EU consumers.
- **Sellers with mixed baskets** where one order contains many different product types, each its own tariff line.
- **Anyone whose EU margins were already thin** after marketplace fees and shipping.

Selling through a marketplace doesn't make the charges disappear. Someone in the chain pays, and if it's the customer at the door, you'll see it in refused deliveries.

One note for Turkish exporters: the Commission's guidance says goods covered by preferential agreements or Customs Union measures can be excluded from the €3 duty in some cases, depending on how they are declared and whether VAT was collected through IOSS. Most small parcels don't go through that route, so check with your forwarder instead of assuming it applies to you. The handling fee is a separate charge either way.

## What I'd do this month

**Recalculate landed cost per product, not per order.** Add €5 per product line to your EU unit economics and see which listings stop working.

**Raise the basket, not just the price.** Bundles, multipacks and free-shipping thresholds make more sense now. Five of the same thing costs the same in fixed charges as one.

**Decide who pays, and say it clearly.** If you collect the charges at checkout, the customer knows the real price upfront. If you don't, make sure they're told before delivery. Surprise fees at the door are the most expensive kind.

**Clean your product data.** Correct tariff codes and clear product descriptions now decide how many "items" a parcel counts as. Messy data can turn one item into three.

**Look again at stock in the EU.** For steady sellers, shipping in bulk to a warehouse or a distributor inside the EU may beat thousands of single parcels. A separate handling fee for goods released from customs warehouses is planned from 2028, and the Commission has said it will be lower. The details aren't set yet, so treat this as a direction, not a plan.

## My take

I don't think this kills small-scale exporting to Europe. I think it ends the model of shipping one cheap item at a time and hoping volume fixes the math. The sellers who do well will be the ones with better baskets, cleaner data and, for their best products, stock closer to the customer.

It's the same lesson I wrote about in [what marketplaces taught me](/blog/marketplaces-taught-me-ai/): when the rules change, sellers with solid fundamentals and clean data adjust, and the ones relying on a trick get caught out. AI can help with the boring part here, like checking tariff codes across a catalogue or recalculating margins for hundreds of SKUs, which is exactly the kind of [unglamorous work it's good at](/blog/boring-ai-wins/).

This isn't legal or customs advice. The rules are still being finalised, so confirm the details with your customs broker or logistics partner before you change anything.
