The value of a trade fair is mostly decided before you arrive and after you leave. Before: build a short target list and book meetings weeks in advance. During: write a two-line note right after every conversation and tag each lead. After: send a personal follow-up within 48 hours, while both of you still remember the talk.

That is the whole method. The rest of this note is how I would do each part, whether you are walking the halls as a visitor or standing at a stand as a salesperson.

## Before the fair: a target list and booked meetings

Most fairs publish an exhibitor list and a hall plan weeks before opening. Use them. Go through the list and split it into three groups: companies you must meet, companies worth a quick visit, and everything else. I think 15-25 names in the first group is plenty for a two or three day fair. More than that and you end up rushing every conversation.

Then contact the first group and ask for a fixed slot. A short email works: who you are, why you want to meet, two or three time options, and your stand or phone number. Many large fairs also run matchmaking tools in their app or website, and visitor guides recommend using them to set up meetings in advance ([Fair Point](https://www.fair-point.com/en/blog/how-to-prepare-for-a-trade-fair-visit?id=23)). A booked 30 minutes with a decision maker beats an hour of hoping someone is at their stand when you walk past.

For each booked meeting, write down one objective. Not "get to know them", but something you can check off: confirm whether they import directly, learn who handles purchasing for a region, get a sample request. Tourism Australia's trade show guidance says the same thing: set a clear objective for each appointment and research the buyer's market before you sit down ([Tourism Australia](https://tourism.australia.com/en/resources/industry-resources/trade-show-tips/meetings-and-follow-up.html)).

Leave gaps in the schedule. The best conversation of the fair is often one you did not plan.

## During the fair: short notes and simple tags

Here is the step people skip. After each conversation, stop for one or two minutes and write a short note before the next one starts. By the end of day two, every face and every business card blurs together. A note written in the aisle is worth more than a perfect memory you think you will have tonight.

Tourism Australia recommends taking notes at every meeting and using a prepared template ([Tourism Australia](https://tourism.australia.com/en/resources/industry-resources/trade-show-tips/meetings-and-follow-up.html)). My template would be four lines:

- Who: name, role, company, country.
- What they need: in their words, not yours.
- Next step: what you promised and what they promised.
- Tag: hot, warm or cold.

Keep the tags simple. Hot means a clear need and a concrete next step. Warm means interest without a defined next step. Cold means nice talk, no fit right now. You can do this in a notes app, a voice memo, or on the back of the business card. A photo of the card plus a 20 second voice note is enough.

If you are working a stand with colleagues, agree on the same template and tags before the fair opens. Otherwise you will spend the week after trying to merge three different note styles.

## After the fair: follow up within 48 hours

Follow-up timing is where most of the value is lost. Tourism Australia calls timing "imperative" and advises following up during the show or immediately after, starting with your highest priority leads ([Tourism Australia](https://tourism.australia.com/en/resources/industry-resources/trade-show-tips/meetings-and-follow-up.html)). I use 48 hours as a working rule because it is short enough that the other person still remembers you, and long enough to get home and sit at a desk.

Start with the hot tags. Each follow-up should mention one specific thing from your note: the product they asked about, the market they mentioned, the question you could not answer at the stand. Then attach exactly what you promised and propose one next step with a date. A generic "nice to meet you at the fair" email reads like a mass mailing, because it usually is one.

Warm leads get a shorter message within the same week. Cold leads go into your contact list with their note, so the next time you meet them you know where you left off.

This is also a good place for a little automation. Turning your fair notes into draft follow-up emails is exactly the kind of boring task AI does well, as I wrote in [how AI fits into export sales](/blog/ai-and-export-sales/). You still read and edit every draft before sending.

## What I would measure

Do not measure the number of cards collected. Measure how many meetings you booked in advance, how many follow-ups went out within 48 hours, and how many leads reached a real next step, like a sample, a quote or a call, within a month. Those three numbers tell you whether the fair was worth the trip.

If you are a Turkish exporter, also check the fair support rules from the Ministry of Trade before you book a stand, since applications have their own deadlines after the fair ([Ticaret Bakanlığı](https://ticaret.gov.tr/destekler/ihracat-destekleri/5973-sayili-ihracat-destekleri-hakkinda-karara-iliskin-genelgeler/yurt-disi-ve-yurt-ici-fuar-destekleri)).
